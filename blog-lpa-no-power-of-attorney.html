What happens if your parent loses capacity without an LPA in place

By the ClearNest team · April 2026

Every year, families across the UK face the same painful discovery. A parent has a stroke, a fall, or a sudden diagnosis — and nobody has legal authority to access their accounts, manage their property, or make decisions on their behalf.

A Lasting Power of Attorney is a legal document that allows a trusted person to step in and manage someone's finances and welfare if they lose the mental capacity to do so themselves. Without one, families have no automatic right to act — regardless of how close the relationship is.

According to a Freedom of Information request submitted to the Ministry of Justice and published by Which?, more than 50,000 LPA applications were rejected by the Office of the Public Guardian in the 2023 to 2024 financial year. The most common reasons were not complicated. Missing signatures or dates. The incorrect signing order — the donor must sign first, then the certificate provider, then the attorneys. Incorrect witnesses. Contradictory instructions. Small errors with significant consequences.

The rejection rate has been rising sharply. Figures obtained by MoneyWeek via a Freedom of Information request to the Office of the Public Guardian show a 199% rise in LPA rejections in England and Wales between 2021 and 2024.

Families without an LPA in place when something goes wrong must apply to the Court of Protection instead. The application fee alone is £408. A professional deputy's fixed costs for the first year of management are £2,116 plus VAT, according to Practice Direction 19B published by the Judiciary of England and Wales in April 2024. The process can also take months — time during which families may have no legal access to a parent's accounts at all.

The most important detail is one most families only discover too late. An LPA can only be registered while the person still has mental capacity. Once capacity is lost, the window closes permanently.

What you can do right now is straightforward. Find out whether your parent has an LPA in place. If they do, confirm where it is registered and who the named attorneys are. If they do not, the right time to start is before there is any urgency — because urgency is exactly when it becomes impossible.
